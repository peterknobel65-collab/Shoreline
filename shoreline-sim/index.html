Laulea Shoreline Lab

A physics model of the eroding pocket beach in front of the white house on Laulea Place, driven by real NOAA wave-buoy and tide records. Use it to compare legal, soft ways to stabilise the shoreline: native dune plants, sand nourishment and coir (coconut-fibre) toe protection. You can also test what large winter swells, king tides and sea-level rise do to each option.

1 · Site reading

What the photos and aerial show

A short unprotected gap between two rock wallsAbout 34 m of natural shoreline in front of the white house sits between large basalt revetments on both neighbouring lots. Waves wrap around the ends of walls and scour the unprotected stretch next to them. This is called flanking.
An active erosion scarpA near-vertical cut 0.5–1 m tall through dark soil and coral rubble, with roots and white irrigation pipe hanging out. Fresh debris lies at its base, so waves reached it in the last few weeks.
Stripped winter beachLittle sand is left against the scarp. A lag of cobbles and coral rubble lies on the upper beach, and loose basalt boulders are scattered across it. The boulders are probably stones displaced from the old wall.
Exposed to NW winter swellThe beach faces about 10° (north). Big North Pacific swells arrive from 315–335°, roughly 45° off the shore line, and long-period ones (17–22 s) give the highest runup.
Reef and rock bench offshoreDark reef and rock patches nearshore break waves before they reach the sand. This protection is the largest uncertainty in the model.
Good plants already presentThe berm is covered in pōhuehue (beach morning glory), a native sand binder. Its shallow runners can't hold the bank once waves undercut it.

4 · Recommendation (draft)

A living shoreline, done together with the neighbours

The model and the site point the same way. The bank fails because there is too little sand in front of it, and because it is a short gap between two walls. Plants alone slow the cutting but cannot stop it. Sand placed against the bank does stop it while the sand lasts. The pocket beach spreads any sand along its whole 110 m length within weeks of the first big swell, so sand placed only in front of your lot ends up helping the neighbours too.

  1. Now (this winter): photograph and measure the scarp monthly from fixed points; cap and reroute the exposed irrigation pipes; keep foot traffic off the bank. Ask OCCL about temporary erosion control only if the bank gets within a few metres of the house.
  2. Survey (next 1–2 months): a licensed surveyor's beach profile and certified shoreline. Those elevations replace the estimates in this model.
  3. Permit (3–9 months): apply to DLNR-OCCL for small-scale beach nourishment, ideally as a joint application with 459 and 463 covering the whole pocket. Volume and timing will come from the recalibrated model.
  4. Build (late spring–summer, calm season): place compatible sand as a dune against the bank, add buried coir envelopes at its core if approved, and plant natives densely.
  5. Maintain: add sand every 3–5 years using the maintenance volume in the permit; replant after big winters; re-run this model with each new survey.

Native plants for the dune

  • ʻAkiʻaki (Sporobolus virginicus): low grass, dense roots, binds sand at the front of the dune.
  • Pōhuehue (Ipomoea pes-caprae): already on site; long runners trap blowing sand.
  • Naupaka kahakai (Scaevola taccada): shrub on the dune crest, deep woody roots.
  • Hinahina kū kahakai (Heliotropium anomalum), nanea (Vigna marina), ʻilima (Sida fallax): fill in and hold the surface.

Plant on the existing lawn edge and on placed sand, landward of the certified shoreline unless your permit says otherwise.

Avoid

  • New seawalls, revetments or rock piles. State law now prohibits private shoreline hardening where there is a sand beach.
  • Moving the loose boulders, or pushing beach sand up against the bank, without asking OCCL first. The beach is state land, and taking or moving beach sand is restricted.
  • Planting to push the shoreline seaward. Planted vegetation is not supposed to set the shoreline location.
  • Plastic sandbags or geotextile left in place long-term. These need a time-limited permit and removal.

6 · Method

How the model works

Waves. Hourly significant wave height, peak period and direction from NOAA buoy 51205 (PacIOOS/CDIP 187), about 9 mi off Pauwela. Waves are refracted toward the shore normal with Snell's law, then cut by a reef transmission factor.

Water level. Measured hourly water level at Kahului Harbor (NOAA 1615680), so seasonal highs, king tides and surge are real. Sea-level rise is added on top.

Runup. Stockdon et al. (2006) 2%-exceedance runup from wave height, period and beach slope. Total water level is tide plus sea-level rise plus runup.

Impact regime. Sallenger (2000): swash (below the bank), collision (hitting the bank), overwash (over the lawn edge).

Erosion. Larson, Erikson & Hanson (2004) wave-impact model, dV/dt = 4·Cs·(TWL − ztoe)²/T. Sand in front of the bank goes first. The cohesive, rooted bank erodes at a calibrated fraction of that rate, reduced further by plant roots. Retreat equals eroded volume divided by bank height.

Sand budget. Sand stripped off the beach moves offshore. Most of it returns in calm weather and over summer; part leaves the pocket for good. That loss is the chronic erosion.

Nourishment spreading. Pelnard-Considère diffusion spreads placed sand along the 110 m pocket. The rock walls at each end keep it inside.

Calibration. The bank's erodibility is tuned so that “do nothing” matches an observed long-term retreat rate. That rate is 0.3 m/yr until we have yours.

Tests: npm test checks the runup formula against a hand calculation, sand conservation, calibration accuracy, and that more protection never produces more retreat.

7 · Open questions

What would sharpen the model most

  1. Which lot is yours: the white house labelled 461 between the two rock walls?
  2. How far has the bank moved, and when? Old photos or a date when the lawn edge was at a known object give the calibration rate.
  3. Do you have a certified shoreline or topographic survey? Elevations of the bank toe, lawn edge and house floor matter most.
  4. Did the scattered boulders come from your property or a neighbour's wall, and was that wall permitted?
  5. Are 459 and 463 interested in a joint project? Sand placed in front of one lot spreads across all three.
  6. Rough budget and timeline? Sand volume and how often to top it up drive cost.
